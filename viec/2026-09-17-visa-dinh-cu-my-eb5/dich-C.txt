[s176] Source of funds
[s177] What counts as a lawful source of funds for EB-5?
[s178] It is investment capital that can be shown to come from lawful sources: income, business profits, the sale of assets, dividends, inheritance, gifts, or a qualifying loan
[s179] USCIS looks not only at where the money came from, but also at whether the entire evidentiary record is consistent, credible, and fully documented
[s180] What is the path of funds?
[s181] It is the process of documenting how the money moved from its original source to the EB-5 investment account
[s182] For example, proceeds from a real estate sale require proof of ownership, the purchase and sale agreement, payment records, tax filings (if applicable), bank statements, and wire transfer records
[s183] The clearer the path of funds, the lower the risk of receiving a Request for Evidence (RFE)
[s184] Can I use gifted funds, an inheritance, real estate sale proceeds, or a loan?
[s185] Yes, provided both the source of funds and the path of funds are fully documented
[s186] Gifted or inherited funds: you need to document the donor's source of funds or the origin of the inherited assets
[s187] Loan proceeds: the collateral, the loan terms, and the USCIS requirements in effect at the time of filing all need to be reviewed
[s188] Families should have their source of funds assessed before transferring any capital
[s189] Projects and the USCIS process
[s190] How does a regional center investment differ from a direct EB-5 investment?
[s191] Direct EB-5 investment: the investor creates and documents direct jobs within their own business
[s192] Regional center investment: the investor contributes capital (US$800,000) to a project managed and funded through a regional center, creating 10 jobs for U.S. workers
[s193] Better suited to families who want to immigrate but do not want to run a business in the United States themselves
[s194] What is Form I-956F?
[s195] Form I-956F is the regional center project application a regional center files to obtain USCIS approval of an EB-5 investment project
[s196] A project with a clear or approved Form I-956F makes it easier for investors to assess its compliance
[s197] Form I-956F does not replace due diligence on financial risk, job-creation risk, and the project's ability to return capital
[s198] What is Form I-526E?
[s199] Form I-526E is the immigrant petition filed by an EB-5 investor who invests through a regional center project
[s200] The petition typically covers the investor's background, source of funds, path of funds, the investment, and the EB-5 project
[s201] Once Form I-526E is approved, the family proceeds with immigrant visa processing through the National Visa Center (NVC) and the consulate, or with the appropriate procedure if they are in the United States and eligible
[s202] What is Form I-829, and when is it filed?
[s203] Form I-829 is the petition to remove conditions on an EB-5 green card
[s204] The investor must file Form I-829 during the 90-day period before the 2-year conditional green card expires
[s205] USCIS reviews whether the investment has been sustained as required and whether the project has created enough qualifying jobs
[s206] How many jobs must an EB-5 project create?
[s207] Each EB-5 investor must be linked to at least 10 qualifying full-time jobs for U.S. workers under EB-5 rules
[s208] For regional center investments, jobs may be counted using an economic model — direct, indirect, and induced jobs — provided the requirements are met
[s209] What is a job cushion?
[s210] A job cushion is the number of projected jobs above the minimum required for all EB-5 investors in the project
[s211] The larger the job cushion, the greater the project's margin for meeting its job-creation target
[s212] It is an important criterion in project due diligence, but it does not ensure I-829 approval
[s213] How do I choose the right EB-5 project?
[s214] Investors should weigh 5 sets of criteria together:
[s215] Job-creation capacity and job cushion
[s216] Targeted Employment Area (TEA) status — rural area or high-unemployment area
[s217] Track record of the regional center and the developer
[s218] Capital structure — collateral — exit strategy
[s219] Transparency of the legal documentation
[s220] The right project must serve both your visa objectives and your management of capital risk.
[s221] Family and residence
[s222] Who can be included in an EB-5 petition?
[s223] The principal applicant's lawful spouse and unmarried children under 21
[s224] If a child is close to 21, the family should review the child's age under the Child Status Protection Act (CSPA) and current visa availability as early as possible to reduce the risk of the child aging out while the case is pending
[s225] Should we file for EB-5 if our child is nearly 21?
[s226] Families with children aged 18 to nearly 21 should assess EB-5 as early as possible
[s227] A child's age for immigration purposes depends not only on their date of birth but also on when Form I-526E is filed, how long the petition is pending, the Visa Bulletin when a visa becomes available, and whether the family seeks to acquire the visa within 1 year of it becoming available
[s228] These cases should be reviewed individually by an immigration attorney
[s229] What happens after Form I-526E is approved?
[s230] Once USCIS approves Form I-526E and a visa is available, the family typically proceeds through the National Visa Center (NVC): filing Form DS-260, preparing civil documents, completing the medical exam, and attending the consular interview
[s231] Applicants already in the United States should have an attorney review their eligibility for adjustment of status under current policy
[s232] If I am already in the United States, can I apply for a green card there?
[s233] It depends on your current immigration status, when you file, and USCIS policy at the time your case is processed
[s234] A May 22, 2026 USCIS update indicates that adjustment of status (AOS) will be treated as a remedy reserved for exceptional circumstances
[s235] It should not be assumed that investors in the United States can always use concurrent filing — each case needs to be assessed by an immigration attorney
[s236] Once we receive EB-5 visas, does the whole family have to enter the United States together?
[s237] The principal applicant must enter the United States before or together with the derivative family members — derivative family members should not enter first
[s238] Everyone must enter before the expiration date printed on the visa (usually up to 6 months from issuance, and possibly sooner if the medical exam expires earlier)
[s239] You need to pay the USCIS Immigrant Fee for USCIS to issue the physical green card (actual timing depends on USCIS and can take up to 90 days)
[s240] Children should be mindful of the age rules if they are not protected under the CSPA
[s241] What is the 2-year conditional EB-5 green card?
[s242] After entering the United States on an EB-5 visa or being granted permanent resident status, the investor typically receives a 2-year conditional green card
[s243] Before this green card expires, the investor must file Form I-829 to remove the conditions
[s244] Once Form I-829 is approved, the family receives unconditional permanent resident status
[s245] Do we have to live near the EB-5 project? How long must we stay in the United States after receiving green cards?
[s246] Living near the project site is not required for EB-5 investors — the family can live, study, and work in any U.S. state
[s247] Permanent residents must maintain the intent to reside permanently in the United States — an absence of more than 6 months may prompt additional questions at the port of entry
[s248] Staying outside the United States for more than 1 year, or beyond the validity of a reentry permit, may require a returning resident (SB-1) visa — and carries a risk of being found to have abandoned residence
[s249] Families should maintain a U.S. address, tax filings, bank accounts, insurance, school enrollment, and other genuine ties to the United States
[s250] How soon can I apply for U.S. citizenship?
[s251] Permanent residents can apply for U.S. naturalization after 5 years if they meet the following requirements:
[s252] Continuous residence
[s253] Sufficient physical presence
[s254] Good moral character
[s255] English and civics requirements
[s256] Extended travel can affect naturalization eligibility — families should plan their residence from the moment they receive their green cards.

[s258] Do EB-5 green card holders have U.S. tax obligations?
[s259] U.S. permanent residents are generally treated as U.S. tax residents under the green card test: holding a green card makes you a U.S. tax resident, and you may be required to file U.S. tax returns on your worldwide income, depending on your circumstances

[s261] Consult a U.S. tax advisor both before and after receiving your green card
